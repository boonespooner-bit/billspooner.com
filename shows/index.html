Live · 1970 – 1983

The bills.

Led Zeppelin at Kezar. KISS and Rush at Winterland. Frank Zappa at Knebworth. David Bowie in front of sixty thousand people. The shows The Tubes played, and the bands whose names shared the marquee.

Prologue · Before The Tubes 1970

Trinity Cathedral

Phoenix, Arizona · June 1970
  • The BeansBill Spooner’s band

Two years before The Tubes existed, Bill Spooner was running The Beans out of Phoenix with Vince Welnick on keys. This poster is one of the few surviving artifacts from that era — the band would load into a truck for San Francisco, merge with the Red, White & Blues Band, and come out the other side as something else entirely.

Art Institute · 1972
One of the first 1972

San Francisco Art Institute

San Francisco, California
  • The TubesCafeteria · art show

The band formed on March 22, 1972, and one of the very first Tubes shows was in the Art Institute cafeteria — playing an art show mounted by a classmate of Prairie Prince and Michael Cotten named Kathryn Bigelow, who would go on to direct The Hurt Locker and become the first woman to win the Academy Award for Best Director.

The Village · 1973
The audition 1973

The Village

San Francisco, California
  • The TubesHeadliner · 3 nights

Journey’s manager Herbie Herbert cut a deal with Bill Graham: sell out three local shows, and Graham would hand over an opening slot of Herbert’s choosing. The band booked a club called the Village and sold it out three times with themed nights built on post-hippie San Francisco — The Streaker’s Ball and Mondo Bondage. Earlier still, they had played bills with The Pointer Sisters and Sylvester. Herbert collected on the bet by picking the biggest show on the calendar.

BGP handbill · “supporting acts to be announced”

Bill Graham Presents handbill, Kezar Stadium, Golden Gate Park, 1973

The break 1973

Kezar Stadium

San Francisco, California · June 2, 1973
  • Led ZeppelinHeadliner
  • Roy HarperSupport
  • The TubesSupport
  • Lee MichaelsSupport

The show that changed everything — and nearly ended everything. Two years before their first record deal, The Tubes walked onto a stadium stage in their own backyard ahead of Led Zeppelin at the peak of the band’s powers. Fee Waybill went out as an early version of Quay Lewd, hurling flour and candy into the crowd as “cocaine” and “pills,” and the crowd threw it back. Bill Graham was furious, telling manager Herbie Herbert the band would never play San Francisco again. He got over it: within a year Graham was booking them at Winterland for New Year’s and Halloween.

The handbill tells you exactly where the band stood that spring. It advertises Led Zeppelin, Kezar Stadium, Saturday 2 June, gates at ten, Zeppelin on at two, six dollars in advance and seven at the gate — and underneath, in place of a lineup: “plus supporting acts to be announced.” The Tubes were one of those unannounced acts. Four hours later they had played to fifty thousand people and Bill Graham was threatening to run them out of San Francisco.

London · November 1977

Six sold-out nights at the Hammersmith Odeon — and a council that tried to stop them.

The Tubes · 1977

Bag-O-Bucks Management / A&M Records, 1977 · public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

The invasion 1977

Hammersmith Odeon

London, England · November 1977 · nights of the 11th and 16th documented
  • The TubesHeadliner · 6 nights

The Tubes booked a week at one of London’s most storied theatres and sold out all six nights — a run the band’s manager Rikki Farr had promoted, with some cheek, as “America’s Answer to Punk.” The shows were recorded across November 1977 and released as What Do You Want From Live the following February: the stage show at its absolute peak, ending each night with Quay Lewd crushed to death under a giant stack of fake speakers. NME’s verdict on the tour became the band’s epitaph in Britain — “They came, they outraged, they conquered.”

Portsmouth · 11.77
Banned 1977

Portsmouth Guildhall

Portsmouth, England · November 1977
  • The TubesCancelled by the council

Scheduled for Remembrance Sunday, and that was the end of it. Portsmouth council sent officials all the way to Newcastle to watch the tour’s opening night and decide for themselves whether the show could go ahead. It could not. The ban made national news, which did the ticket sales everywhere else no harm at all.

Official concert programme, Knebworth, 9 September 1978 · scans via the AFKA Zappa scrapbook archive

The running order · Tubes last
The festival 1978

Knebworth Park

Hertfordshire, England · September 9, 1978
  • Frank ZappaJoint headliner
  • The TubesJoint headliner · closed the show
  • Peter GabrielSupport
  • The Boomtown RatsSupport
  • Dave EdmundsSupport
  • Nick LoweSupport
  • Wilko Johnson’s Solid SendersSupport

Billed, gloriously, as “Oh God, Not Another Boring Old Knebworth.” Zappa and The Tubes were announced as joint headliners — the official programme puts both names on one line, at the same size. The running order printed inside settles who finished the night: Wilko Johnson, Rockpile, the Boomtown Rats, Peter Gabriel, Frank Zappa, and then The Tubes, closing the festival in front of roughly 45,000 people. Peter Gabriel spent his set climbing the stage scaffolding with a large stuffed panda. One of the great strange bills of the decade, and the high-water mark of the band’s standing in Britain.

Europe · after Hammersmith 1977

Chateau Neuf

Oslo, Norway · 29 November 1977
  • The TubesHeadliner

Straight off the Hammersmith run, the band pushed on into Europe. This photograph from the Oslo show is one of the few freely licensed images of The Tubes on stage anywhere — the full production still intact, weeks before the tour turned into a front-page argument about what British councils would allow.

Pantages · 1978
The night Cher ran 1978

Pantages Theatre

Hollywood, California
  • The TubesHeadliner · sold-out run

A sold-out Hollywood run that drew Cher, Gene Simmons, Kate Jackson and the cast of Laverne & Shirley. The set by then included a staged bomb-threat routine called “The Terrorists of Rock” — convincing enough that Cher fled the theatre believing it was real. She later asked the band to appear on her TV special.

Leicester · 1978
Tour over 1978

Leicester

Leicester, England
  • The TubesHeadliner · cut short

The band went back to Europe to build on the Hammersmith breakthrough and got as far as Leicester, where Fee Waybill fell off the stage and broke his leg. The rest of the tour was cancelled; they returned to play the dates that autumn, and went straight from them to Knebworth.

UK itinerary · May 1979

A&M Records tour advertisement · via the Packet of Three Squeeze archive

Opening night · Glasgow Apollo

Ticket stub, Apollo Glasgow, 11 May 1979 · Straight Music · £4.00

Remote Control tour 1979

UK tour · seven nights at Hammersmith

Glasgow to Brighton · 11–31 May 1979
  • The TubesHeadliner
  • SqueezeGuests

Seventeen dates behind Remote Control, the Todd Rundgren–produced concept record about television eating the culture — and seven straight nights at the Hammersmith Odeon, 21 to 27 May, eighteen months after the six-night run that produced What Do You Want From Live.

Squeeze came along as guests, riding “Cool for Cats,” though the tour ad notes they sat out the two-show nights at Newcastle, Manchester and Birmingham. The full run: Glasgow Apollo, Edinburgh Odeon, Newcastle City Hall, Liverpool Empire, Manchester Odeon, Birmingham Odeon, Bristol Colston Hall, Hammersmith Odeon × 7, Leicester De Montfort Hall, Southampton Gaumont and Brighton Centre. The ad signs off in the album’s own voice: PROGRAM TERMINATED. ENDS.

A surviving stub from the opening night pins it down: Apollo, Glasgow, Friday 11 May 1979, doors at 7.30, presented by Straight Music. A seat in the circle cost £4.

Greek Theatre

Los Angeles, California
  • The TubesHeadliner · 2 nights

One of the more improbable pairings of the era: The Tubes headlining the Greek with Yellow Magic Orchestra — Haruomi Hosono, Ryuichi Sakamoto and Yukihiro Takahashi, then in the middle of inventing most of electronic pop — opening. The show was released on home video in 1982.

1975 – 1980 · The runs

Residencies.

The Tubes were never really a one-night band. A show that needed two dozen crew, a choreographer and a live video feed worked better booked into a theatre for a week. These are the rooms they took over.

1975
The Roxy Theatre
Los Angeles

Sold-out run with the new Kenny Ortega–directed stage show. The one that broke them in LA.

1975
Bimbo’s 365 Club
San Francisco

Home turf. Shows here were taped in full colour for TV and survive in the band’s archive.

1975
The Bottom Line
New York City

The East Coast beachhead, alongside sold-out nights at David Allen’s Boarding House back in SF.

1976
The Shrine Auditorium
Los Angeles

Several sold-out nights on the Young and Rich tour. Mingo Lewis joined the band around this stretch.

1977
The Whisky a Go Go
Los Angeles

A standing weekend engagement, played while the band was recording Now during the week.

1977
Palace of Fine Arts
San Francisco

A month-long run carrying the most elaborate stage show the band had yet built.

1983 · The stadium year

Sixty thousand
people deep.

“She’s a Beauty” hit number 10 on the Billboard Hot 100 in 1983, and the rooms changed overnight. Two shows that summer put The Tubes in front of the largest audiences of their career.

Concert shirt, J.F.K. Stadium, Philadelphia, 4 June 1983

Frontiers tour 1983

John F. Kennedy Stadium

Philadelphia, Pennsylvania · June 4, 1983
  • JourneyHeadliner
  • John CougarSupport
  • Sammy HagarSupport
  • The TubesSupport
  • Bryan AdamsSupport

An all-day stadium bill in the rain. The tour shirt prints the order: Journey over John Cougar, Sammy Hagar, The Tubes and Bryan Adams. The weather was bad enough that the bands initially balked at going on at all over electrocution risk. Most of the footage in the NFL Films documentary Journey: Frontiers and Beyond comes from this show.

Newspaper ad · “The Main Event”

Period newspaper advertisement, Sea Mountain Tours / CFMC 103, Saskatoon

Serious Moonlight 1983

Commonwealth Stadium

Edmonton, Alberta · August 7, 1983
  • David BowieHeadliner
  • Peter GabrielSpecial guest
  • The TubesSpecial guest

The fiftieth date of David Bowie’s Serious Moonlight tour, and by the band’s own account roughly sixty thousand people — the biggest crowd The Tubes ever played to. Peter Gabriel again on the same bill, five years on from Knebworth.

The advertisement bills it as “the concert of the decade,” and the reach was real: a Saskatoon radio station and a coach firm sold a $99 package that bussed fans several hundred miles to Edmonton and home again the same day.

Stranger bookings

And then there
was this.

January 1982

Candlestick Park

The Tubes played the halftime of the San Francisco 49ers playoff game remembered as “The Catch” — and recorded the single “Sports Fans” live on the field while they were there. Crew members are visible on the sidelines in the slow-motion replay.

1979

Royce Hall, UCLA

The Remote Control production — banks of TV monitors and a 35mm film projector, about $12,000 of staging — was tested here with no dress rehearsal. The screens were too small to read and the film wouldn’t sync. The whole show was scrapped.

1983

Kabuki Theatre

An hour-long concert special filmed in San Francisco and broadcast on MTV, directed by Jim Yukich — the band finally meeting the medium their stage show had been predicting since 1975.

1986

The Warfield

A benefit for the daughter of Jane Dornacker — the comedian and Tubes cast member who wrote “Don’t Touch Me There” and died in a helicopter crash reporting traffic. The Tubes played with Todd Rundgren.
